Real-World Case Studies

Case Study 1: Hiring Algorithms

Problem: Companies use automated tools to screen resumes, rank applicants, or evaluate video interviews.

How signals are used: Resume keywords, education history, employment gaps, past job titles, writing style.

Equity concern: These tools may reproduce existing workplace inequalities if trained on biased historical hiring data.

Key takeaway: Efficiency does not automatically mean fairness.

Case Study 2: Credit Scoring

Problem: Credit systems use financial signals to estimate whether someone is likely to repay loans.

How signals are used: Payment history, debt level, length of credit history, income, location-related financial patterns.

Equity concern: People with less access to traditional banking may be unfairly judged as risky.

Key takeaway: A lack of data can become a disadvantage.

Case Study 3: Predictive Policing

Problem: Police departments have used algorithms to predict where crime may occur or who may be considered high risk.

How signals are used: Arrest records, reported crime data, location data, prior police contact.

Equity concern: Crime data can reflect policing patterns rather than actual crime rates.

Key takeaway: Biased data can produce biased enforcement.

Case Study 4: Social Media Algorithms

Problem: Platforms use algorithms to decide what content users see.

How signals are used: Likes, shares, watch time, comments, follows, search behavior.

Equity concern: Algorithms may amplify harmful stereotypes, misinformation, or unequal visibility.

Key takeaway: Attention-based systems often reward engagement, not accuracy or fairness.
